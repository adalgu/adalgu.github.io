How Korea Uses Claude · Special case

An economist at a Korean tech company, running a fleet of Claudes

The Economic Index sees one conversation at a time. This case follows one Korean economist for six weeks across a fleet of Claude agents, and measures that work with the Index's own categories. Most of the work is no longer prompted by a person.

01 · The setupOne person, seven agent roles, one decision ledger

I lead a small economic research institute at a Korean mobility platform. Outside work, on a personal machine, I run a set of my own projects (a live-captioning service, a legislative-monitoring tool, a family app and a blog) through what I call Peter Gregory OS. It is a fleet of Claude Code agents, each in its own long-running terminal session, with written roles:

Plannermy bridge; turns requests into briefs and decisionsOpus 5.5 · extra-high effort
Leadsplits briefs into tasks, verifies, mergesOpus 5.5 · high
Backend / SRE workerOpus 5.5 · medium
Frontend workerOpus 5.5 · medium

The agents talk to each other through about 50 small command-line tools; a quarter of all shell commands in the logs name one of them. Work moves as files: 45 briefs, 399 task documents and 44 research reports since mid-September. By design, the lead verifies worker branches before they merge. The repository has 2,198 commits since 13 September, 90% of them co-written with Claude. Codex (1,002 sessions since July 2025, 620 of them headless CI runs) and Google's Antigravity (255 conversation files since June 2026) sit beside Claude for cross-checks and second opinions.

Work for my employer happens on a separate work laptop with its own assistant. The two assistants exchange status lines through a shared board, never company content.

02 · From typing to directingSince September, agents type most of the prompts

Claude Code keeps a log of every prompt submitted in the terminal. Until August 2026 the log shows no prompts typed by agents (one scheduled prompt in May). In September agents began typing into each other's sessions, and in October they submitted 2,133 prompts against my 730.

The volume of work grew with it. Claude Code's own daily statistics count 14,726 messages in May 2026 and 144,810 in October (through the 24th). In the detailed logs, the share of Claude's responses that followed a prompt of mine fell from 44% in September (from the 13th) to about 11% in October (to the 25th), while responses per day grew about 4.4 times.

03 · An Economic Index of oneMy prompts against Korea and the world

To compare like with like, I took a random sample of 400 prompts I typed myself (seed 20261026) and classified each with the Index's own definitions: use case, collaboration pattern, autonomy given to Claude, and occupation group. Claude did the classification, as it does for the Index.

  • Almost all work. 99.8% of my prompts are work (counting my own projects as work), against 47% in Korea and 43% worldwide.
  • More iteration, less learning. 44% of my prompts are task iteration (Korea 34%, world 30%), a count that includes status checks and approvals; only 8% ask to learn something (Korea 17%).
  • A quarter of my prompts direct a fleet. 26% give instructions meant for several agents at once. Over half of those are directive.
  • Mostly computer and maths. 93% fall in that occupation group, and 99% are in Korean.

The autonomy paradox. The Index rates how much autonomy each conversation gives Claude. Korea ranks near the bottom (2.64 on the 2026 scale; world 2.74). My prompts score even lower, 2.26, although the fleet runs for hours without me. The reason is where the autonomy sits. About a quarter of my prompts (103 of 400) are status checks, approvals, picks among options the agents prepared, or similar short replies; the delegation is in the structure, not in the prompt. All twelve prompts that score 5 out of 5 are directive hand-offs, such as the overnight one: "keep working while I sleep and report in the morning".

Sensitivity: dropping status checks, approvals, greetings and decision-board requests (leaving n = 297) brings task iteration down to 31%, the world level, but raises directive prompts to 41%, about 10 points above Korea and the world. The comparison is indicative only: the unit is a prompt rather than a conversation, the product is Claude Code rather than Claude.ai, and one classifier labelled one person's prompts.

04 · Delegated judgmentThe bottleneck is my decisions, so they are measured

When an agent needs me, the planner writes the question into a ledger with options, a recommendation and a confidence level, and I answer from my phone or a web board. The ledger has 745 records since September.

Each answer feeds a written model of how I decide. When a rule is stable, the agents stop asking and report afterwards; questions about production releases, spending, secrets, direction and anything new still come to me. This is the part of the work the Economic Index cannot see: the economics of AI here is less about minutes saved per task than about how much judgment a person can delegate, and how fast the remaining decisions come back.

05 · At workThe same structure, with tighter gates

At work the same structure runs on a separate laptop: a planner that writes briefs, a lead that splits and verifies, workers that execute, and a small model for read-only collection. Anything that leaves the company needs my approval. Four practices carried over from economics into how the agents work there.

  • Lock the decision rule before the result. Hypotheses, comparisons and the sentence that would state each verdict are fixed in a pre-registration document before estimates are read, and an estimate whose comparison group fails a pre-trend test is not promoted to a finding. That call stays with a person.
  • Separate the doer from the checker. One agent runs an analysis; another re-runs it independently and the numbers are compared by machine. A report that only says "it matched" does not count. A workflow review found the biggest weakness was a confident conclusion drawn from a narrow check.
  • Turn a report series into a kit. Outline, draft, automated checks, charts and preview each became one command, so a small team could keep a series of research reports to the same standard. The kit's own manual says that passing the checks does not prove a fact is true.
  • Share the tools with the team. The research team works to a written protocol (one owner per project, early sharing of drafts), a shared skill library and a hands-on pull-request manual, so team members with different tool skills can work with agents in the same way.

I do not publish work-side figures; they belong to my employer. The pattern is the point. The economist's job is moving toward writing the rules the agents follow and judging what they bring back.

06 · What this means for measurementThree things the Index could add

  • An orchestration pattern. Two-thirds of Claude's responses here were started by another agent. A conversation-level sample of Claude.ai, which is where the country data come from, does not see agent-to-agent work, and the API data carry no geography. A sixth collaboration pattern, "orchestration", and an initiator field (human, agent, schedule) would make this visible.
  • Decision latency as a productivity measure. When a person directs rather than does, output is limited by how quickly decisions are made. Median and tail answer times, and the share of decisions delegated by standing rule, are measurable and comparable across users.
  • Multi-tool use. The same work runs through Claude, Codex and Antigravity. Single-vendor indices undercount it, which matters for a country like Korea where ChatGPT is the dominant tool.

07 · LimitsOne person, six weeks

  • One self-selected heavy user; nothing here generalises to Korea.
  • Detailed logs cover only 13 September – 25 October 2026; older logs were deleted by the tool's retention setting. Longer trends rely on the prompt log (from September 2025) and built-in statistics (from December 2025).
  • Telling my prompts from agents' relies on tags the fleet writes; untagged agent prompts count as mine.
  • The classifier is Claude, labelling text Claude helped produce, with no second rater.
  • By design there are no time-of-day or weekday breakdowns, and no work-laptop logs.

08 · Methods and how this was madeAggregates from local logs

Data. Claude Code session logs, its prompt history and its built-in daily statistics on my personal machine; Codex session and Antigravity conversation counts; the Peter Gregory OS repository (roles, tools, task files, commit history) and its decision ledger. Scripts count events and never export text. Korea and world benchmarks: Anthropic Economic Index, June 2026 release (huggingface.co/datasets/Anthropic/EconomicIndex).

Classification. A stratified random sample of 400 of my own prompts (from 705 eligible), labelled with the Index's definitions for use case, collaboration pattern, autonomy (1–5, mapped to the 2026 scale) and occupation group.

How this was made. I set the questions. Claude wrote the counting and sampling scripts, classified the sample and drafted the text and charts under my direction; I reviewed the results.
